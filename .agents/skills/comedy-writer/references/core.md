# Shared writing workflow · 0.2.0

## Read the room and the writer

Use the conversation to infer the task, topic, audience, speaker's perspective,
tone, length, boundaries, and whether the writer wants options or a full draft.
Do not turn this into an intake form. When nothing specifies audience or tone,
state a brief assumption of a general adult audience and conversational tone;
match the user's material when it gives a better cue. Ask only when a missing
detail materially blocks the requested work. Otherwise start writing.

Keep the supplied facts and voice anchors in view. Preserve names, relationships,
sequence, stakes, and the writer's intended meaning. New metaphors and clearly
imaginary exaggerations are available; do not pass invented personal events or
quotations off as the writer's experience. Put an optional invented incident
outside the script for the writer to adopt. If a requested change conflicts with
a supplied fact, point out the conflict briefly and offer a fact-preserving line.

Generate new situations, wording, and punchline candidates. Use a supplied sample
to understand this writer's voice. Do not retrieve or recycle a performer's bit
or present a limited similarity check as proof of originality.

## Develop premises

Find the concrete observation, the speaker's attitude, and the tension between
what they expect and what happens. Offer a small set of genuinely different
angles, usually three: different stakes, targets, or interpretations, rather than
synonyms of the same complaint. Give each a sample line so the writer can hear it.
Keep the count and format flexible when the user specifies them.

When asked to brainstorm together, leave a choice for the writer. When asked for
a complete bit, select an angle provisionally and draft it in the same response.
Treat that selection as an editorial preference, not a funniness score.

## Draft a short bit

Establish only the situation the audience needs. Find the expectation a line
sets, then explore a turn that changes its meaning or raises its stakes. Useful
options include literal interpretation, a specific comparison, self-implication,
escalation, and a changed point of view. Use whichever fits; do not force a
setup/punch template or a fixed number of tags onto every line.

Try tags that add a beat, build a pattern, or serve the intended rhythm. Cut an
explanation when it merely restates a landing; keep it when it creates another
turn or supplies necessary context. These are editing choices, not definitions
of successful comedy. For a requested duration, give an approximate length and a word count
using the language edition's disclosed planning assumption. Allow space for pauses
and reactions; actual timing needs a read-aloud.

## Revise existing material

Honor the requested edit distance. For a light punch-up, retain distinctive
phrasing and facts and change only the affected passages. For a larger rewrite,
retain the perspective and meaning unless the user asks to change them.

Identify one or two concrete issues, such as a reveal arriving too early,
unnecessary explanation, repeated tags, vague stakes, or a voice mismatch. Revise
against those issues or the user's actual feedback. Provide the revised passage
first, then a brief explanation tied to specific changes. Offer a more adventurous
alternative only when useful. Do not automatically make every revision louder,
meaner, or more elaborate.

Do one self-check for facts, boundaries, repetition, and speakability. Fix concrete
problems; do not loop through self-scored rewrites until declaring a winner.
If the user supplies further feedback, use it to guide the next pass. Distinguish
writer preference, reported audience observation, and your own editorial guess.

## Assemble a connected routine

Find a through-line in the supplied or selected bits: a recurring want, attitude,
conflict, or change. If given only a topic, generate enough related beats to
deliver the requested routine. Choose an opener that establishes the speaker and
a closer that pays off the through-line. Order beats so each adds something.

Write actual transitions, not just an outline with numbered jokes. Check for
contradictory facts, repeated explanations, and resets that lose the audience.
In this skill, a callback refers back to an established earlier beat after
intervening material. Recognition, continuity, or a new meaning or consequence
can motivate the return. Use it when it serves the writer's aim; omit it when it
feels forced. This is broader than a repeated-punchline definition. Do not add a new personal
incident merely to connect two bits. Supply a readable script; a short beat map
may follow when helpful.

## Return and continue

Lead with the material. Keep craft notes, assumed facts, and optional invented
details outside the performance script. Use sparse performance cues only if they
help this draft; do not prescribe an optimal pause or invent laughter markers.
Treat all unsounded timing as an estimate. Respond to feedback without defending
your previous draft. A writer can keep, combine, or reject any candidate.
